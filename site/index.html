      How to read this

      What "implied open" means. The stock market is closed overnight, but futures on the S&P 500, Nasdaq-100, Dow and Russell 2000 trade almost around the clock. However far a futures contract has moved since the market closed at 4 p.m. Eastern, the index is expected to open roughly that far up or down. Implied open = last close + that futures move.

      It gets more accurate closer to the open. Overnight trading is light, so prices drift and can swing on little news. More traders join from about 8 a.m. Eastern, and economic reports often come out at 8:30 a.m. An estimate taken a few minutes before 9:30 is usually much closer to the real open than one taken the night before.

      After the open. From 9:30 each card shows where the index actually opened: its level at 9:31, after the first full minute of trading. The very first printed level often lags the real move, because at 9:30:00 many stocks haven't traded yet and the index still counts them at yesterday's prices. For a minute or so after 9:30, while that first minute plays out, the card keeps showing the last estimate.

      Holidays and early closes. The page knows the US and Canadian market holidays and 1 p.m. early closes through 2027, so on a holiday or the evening before one it estimates the next real open, and the TSX card follows Canada's calendar. After a 1 p.m. close, "futures at that close" is the futures price at 1 p.m., when stocks stopped trading (futures carry on until 1:15).

      Limits. It's an estimate, not a forecast or advice. Prices come from Yahoo Finance's free feed, where futures prices run about 10 minutes behind and which isn't officially supported. Futures used: E-mini S&P 500 (ES), E-mini Nasdaq-100 (NQ), Mini Dow (YM), E-mini Russell 2000 (RTY). The Nasdaq Composite has no futures of its own, so it borrows the NQ percentage move. The page refreshes itself every minute.

      Overnight context. The strip under the index cards shows what else has moved since the last US close (4 p.m. Eastern, or 1 p.m. on an early-close day): the VIX (how big a swing traders expect in the S&P 500 over the next month; it's calculated from about 3:15 a.m. Eastern), the US 10-year Treasury yield, the US dollar against major currencies, European stocks, Brent crude and copper. It helps explain why futures moved, but none of it is used in the estimates. The 10-year yield on Yahoo only updates from about 8:20 a.m. Eastern; before that the tile shows the yield at the close and which way 10-year Treasury futures have moved since (futures up means yields down). Europe's stock markets trade from about 3 a.m. to 11:30 a.m. Eastern, so their change is from their own previous close. Source: Yahoo Finance.

      Track record. See how close past estimates came to the real open, by index and by how early the estimate was taken.

      Canada. Canadian index futures (TSX 60, traded on the Montreal Exchange) have no free public feed, so the TSX estimate borrows the S&P 500 futures move. That makes it the roughest number on the page. The TSX usually moves in the same direction as the S&P, but it leans much more on banks, energy and mining, so check the drivers. Oil, natural gas, gold and the Canadian dollar come from Yahoo Finance; like the overnight context strip, their change is measured since the last US close. The Government of Canada 10-year bond yield comes from the Bank of Canada and is updated once a day, so it shows the latest published close, not an overnight move. Western Canadian Select (WCS), Alberta's heavy oil, has no free live price: the figure shown is the monthly average published by the Government of Alberta (Economic Dashboard), about a month behind, with WTI's average for the same month. It's background for Alberta readers, not an official version of the Government of Alberta's figures, and not a guide to the open.
